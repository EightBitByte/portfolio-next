*My NAS Homepage (Dashwise).*

I recently graduated from UC Irvine, and along with it, I received some gift 
money. I was tempted on blowing it on Steam games or PC Parts, but I instead 
decided to be a responsible adult and invest in my future by securing my data 
with a NAS[^1]. I started self-hosting ever since and haven't looked back.

# Motivation
The primary motivator behind the NAS, aside from wanting to start homelabbing 
for the longest time, was subscriptions. I was coming up on my Google One 
subscription deadline, and I had 100+ GB of photos stored up. I forget how 
much now, but I believe the monthly subscription is something outrageous, 
like 20 bucks a month[^2]. This simply would not do.

# Hardware
As such, I took things into my own hands. I purchased a NAS[^3] for a couple 
hundred bucks, along with 2x 2TB drives and 2x 8GB SO-DIMMs (optional, for 
more docker containers) to slot into it. Taking this thing apart and slotting 
the components was fun and fairly simple, even if you're not a computer-
building fan like me.

Plugging it in and booting it up for the first time, I set up the SSH keys 
and configured the on-board web interface. Now, the real fun begins.

# Docker is Amazing, What do I Host?
I've used Docker in the past for development reasons, for packaging and 
running applications for shipping. I loved it then as a developer, and I love 
it all the more now as a user.

If you're not familiar, [Docker](https://www.docker.com/) is a containerizer. 
In essence, it provides each application its own little sandbox to play 
around in, to store its data and run and whatnot. I can go on and on about 
what makes Docker such a godsend, but I'll save you the reading; it just 
makes things more convenient.

I opened up a new `Docker` directory on `/volume1/` and got to composing some containers.

## Immich: Google Photos, but better
![Immich promo image.](/immich-preview.webp)

[Immich](https://immich.app/) was my first container. It's a self-hostable, 
open-source[^4] server and web-application that allows you to take control of 
your photos. It provides everything you could need out of the box: video and 
image uploading with Google Takeout import support, facial recognition, album 
creation, OCR, and more. It even employs Material UI, so if you've used 
Google Photos for any length of time, you'll feel right at home. I love this 
app, and the security of knowing my photos are safe and backed-up, within 
reach, is a nice feeling.

## Nextcloud: A Virtual Drive
![Nextcloud promo image.](/nextcloud-preview.webp)

[NextCloud](https://nextcloud.com/) does what it says on the tin. It provides 
a Google-Drive-like experience, allowing you to browse and upload files 
easily through a web-interface. It also, much to my satisfaction, implements 
a webDAV interface, which allowed me to automatically sync my Obsidian vault 
to the NAS. In that sense, it was also a replacement for GitHub, which I had 
been using previously to sync my vault. Overall, awesome app, removes any 
need for Google Drive.

## Actual Budget: Budgeting without Hassle
![Actual Budget promo image.](/actual-preview.webp)

[Actual Budget](https://actualbudget.org) allows you to set clear boundaries on what you 
want to spend per month, allows you easily import transactions or sync with 
most major banks, and categorizes transactions automatically with their Rules 
feature. I personally really like the charts and visual features of the app, 
which help me see my spending in real-time. (*too much In-N-Out and Chick-Fil-A...*)

## Karakeep: Bookmarks, Moodboards, etc.
![Karakeep promo image.](/karakeep-preview.webp)

For the longest time, I've been looking for a way to aggregate things on the 
internet that I find interesting or to save for later reference. [Karakeep](
https://karakeep.app/) comes in real clutch here, allowing me to paste links 
and photos, as well as categorize and store important webstuffs. I frequently 
use it while browsing web design forums, art pages, and for helpful 
courses/infographics.

## FreshRSS: Goodbye Twitter, YouTube, and more!
![FreshRSS promo image.](/freshrss-preview.webp)

[FreshRSS](https://freshrss.org) is a simple web-app and RSS aggregator.
I was looking for a bare-bones, simple-to-use RSS reader. All of the other RSS
feed readers I found were too flashy, didn't work, or implemented unnecessary
features (like, why do I want an AI to summarize the content? no thanks). 
FreshRSS has everything I want, nothing I don't. 
[RSSHub](https://rsshub.rssforever.com/) is a nice companion app. It allows
you to RSS-ify places that might have a feed, like GitHub, Telegram, and 
Twitter. These two help me unplug from social media almost entirely.

## Honorable Mentions: Tailscale, Backblaze
These two, [Tailscale](https://tailscale.com) and [Backblaze B2](https://backblaze.com) are 
the backbone of my NAS experience. They're not flashy and exciting like the 
previous apps, but are arguably the most important. 

Tailscale provides a VPN-like experience by creating what they dub a "tailnet," 
which is your own, "[...] secure, interconnected collection of users, devices, 
and resources," ([Tailscale Docs](https://tailscale.com/docs/concepts/tailnet)).
It's awesome, because it allows you to access your NAS from anywhere without 
needing to expose a port to the internet. No security risk, no hassle, all easy.

Backblaze is not so much an app as it is a service. They provide cheap data 
backups. I wrote a simple bash script that `cron` runs nightly on my data to 
back up to the Backblaze servers. It's important to keep your data backed up 
as much as you can[^5], and Backblaze helps me achieve that 
comfortably.

# I Love Self-Hosting
For the many, many reasons listed above and many more I've forgotten to list, 
I love self-hosting. It's made life easier, made my data more secure, saved 
me money, keeps me organized, and provided me with a new hobby. Check out [
selfh.st](https://self.hst) for more cool apps, and let me know of your self-hosting 
experiences (or plans, or questions)!
